  -----------------                 ------------                   ----------------
  LaTeX / PDF                       Typed graph of                 Lean 4 / Coq

  equations inline,                 (nodes) with                   axiom or deduction
  figures, citations                typed edges                    rule; fully
                                    between them                   machine-checkable

The current implementation is a FLAT graph: a bag of nodes with a bag of edges.
There is no hierarchy inside nodes (theorems are opaque text blobs) and no
nesting of nodes inside other nodes (beyond parent_id pointers).


A.2. THE GRAPH: PaperGraph
================================================================================

  PaperGraph
  +-- paper_id: str
  +-- objects: Dict[str, MathObject]   flat dict keyed by ID
  +-- relations: List[Relation]        flat list of edges

All objects live at the same level. An equation "inside" a theorem is just
another object in the flat dict, linked by a has_part edge.

B.1. DESIGN PRINCIPLES
================================================================================

1. CONTAINMENT TREE + SEMANTIC GRAPH

   The representation is TWO structures overlaid on the same set of nodes:

   (a) A TREE of containment ("children" lists).
       Every node has zero or more children. A theorem contains its
       assumptions, conclusions, and embedded equations. A proof contains
       its steps. A step may contain sub-steps or case branches.
       There is exactly one parent per node (tree, not DAG).

   (b) A GRAPH of semantic relations ("relations" list).
       Cross-cutting edges like "uses", "generalizes", "cites" that can
       point anywhere in the tree -- within the same paper or across papers.

   This is the same architecture as:
   - DOM (tree of HTML elements + hyperlink graph)
   - AST with cross-references (scope tree + symbol table)
   - OMDoc (theory > statement > formula hierarchy + import/proof-ref graph)

   Why not just a graph?  Pure graphs lose the part-whole nesting that is
   essential to mathematical structure. "Equation E3 is inside Proof P1
   which proves Theorem T2" is a containment fact, not a semantic relation.

   Why not just a tree?  Trees can't express "Proof P1 uses Lemma L3" or
   "Theorem T1 generalizes Theorem T2" -- these are cross-links.

2. NODES HAVE INTERNAL STRUCTURE

   A theorem is not an opaque blob. It is a node with typed children:
   assumptions (a list), conclusions (a list), embedded equations, etc.
   These children are themselves nodes in the tree.

3. MULTIPLE GRANULARITY LEVELS

   The tree has a natural depth:

   Level 0: Paper
   Level 1: Sections (optional structural grouping)
   Level 2: Blocks -- theorem, definition, proof, algorithm, remark, example, misc
   Level 3: Sub-parts -- assumption, conclusion, proof-step, case, def-clause, equation
   Level 4: Atoms -- term, symbol, citation reference

   Not every paper needs all levels. The representation is a "fill what you
   can" structure: a shallow parse might only populate levels 0+2; a deep
   parse (or LLM) can fill levels 3+4.

4. PARSING-INDEPENDENT SCHEMA

   The schema defines what CAN be represented, not what WILL be parsed.
   Some fields will be empty for most papers. That is fine -- the schema
   should be rich enough to represent a perfectly annotated paper, even if
   the automated parser can only fill 30% of the fields.

5. GRACEFUL DEGRADATION (fill what you can, skip what you can't)

   Every level of the hierarchy is OPTIONAL. The parser can stop at any
   depth when confidence is too low. The JSON simply omits the key or
   uses null/empty list. The schema never REQUIRES a field below the
   block level (id, type, raw_latex are always required).

   Degradation ladder (from least to most detail):

   Level 0 -- BLOCK ONLY (always achievable by heuristic parser):
     A block has: id, type, raw_latex, clean_text.
     No internal structure. This is the floor -- every block has this.

   Level 1 -- METADATA (achievable by regex/heuristic):
     Add: label, number, name, strategy (for proofs), proves (for proofs).
     Theorems remain opaque text blobs.

   Level 2 -- ASSUMPTIONS / CONCLUSIONS (heuristic + simple NLP):
     Theorem/lemma blocks get assumptions: [...] and conclusions: [...].
     Proofs get a flat list of steps: [{index, text}].
     Definitions get definiendum, genus.
     Each is a list of text strings -- no further structure inside them.

   Level 3 -- LAMPORT PROOF STRUCTURE (requires LLM or deep parsing):
     Proof steps get: assume, prove, justification, sub_steps, cases.
     Steps can nest recursively. This is the richest level.
     Assumptions/conclusions get optional "formal" semi-formal notation.

   Level 4 -- EQUATION EXTRACTION (requires equation matching):
     Display equations are extracted as EquationBlock children of their
     parent block. Orphan equations go under a MiscBlock catch-all.
     Equations are never top-level blocks.

   A SINGLE PAPER can have blocks at DIFFERENT levels. For example:
     - Theorem T1 is at Level 2 (assumptions extracted)
     - Proof R1 is at Level 0 (couldn't parse steps -- just raw text)
     - Theorem T2 is at Level 1 (has label/number but no assumptions)
     - Proof R2 is at Level 3 (LLM extracted full Lamport structure)

   IMPLEMENTATION RULE:
     Every field below Level 0 that is absent/null/empty means "not
     extracted" -- NOT "doesn't exist." The distinction matters:
     a theorem with assumptions: [] means "we tried and found none"
     or "we didn't try." To distinguish, check the provenance block
     which records what parser/LLM was used. If provenance.parser is
     "heuristic_v1.2" and assumptions is missing, it means the
     heuristic couldn't extract them. If provenance.llm_model is set
     and assumptions is [], the LLM explicitly found no assumptions.


B.2. PRIOR ART AND INFLUENCES
================================================================================

The proposed design draws from several sources:

  OMDoc (Kohlhase, 1998-2012)
    Three-level XML hierarchy: formula < statement < theory.
    Statements include: definition, axiom, assertion (theorem/lemma/...),
    proof, example. Proofs have proof steps with local goals and
    justifications. Theories group related statements with import/export.
    Ref: https://www.omdoc.org/format/

  Lamport Structured Proofs (1993, 2012)
    Proofs as hierarchical numbered steps. Each step has:
    ASSUME (local hypotheses), PROVE (local goal), and justification.
    Sub-proofs nest to arbitrary depth. Keywords: SUFFICES, PICK, CASE.
    The scoping of assumptions mirrors variable scoping in programming.
    Ref: https://lamport.azurewebsites.net/pubs/proof.pdf

  MMLKG (Mizar Mathematical Library Knowledge Graph, 2023)
    60k+ nodes, 300k+ edges. Node types include definitions, theorems,
    proof steps. Edges: MEMBER (part-of), RELATED (uses/references),
    BROADER (generalization). Stored in Neo4j property graph.
    Ref: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41597-023-02681-3

  Mathematical Knowledge Graphs with OWL ontology
    Root class MathematicalEntity with subclasses: Theorem, Definition,
    Axiom, Lemma, Corollary, ProofStep. Reasoning methods (Deduction,
    Induction) modeled under MathematicalStep.
    Ref: https://www.mdpi.com/2673-3951/6/3/53

  Existing Gold Standard in this project (gold/1805.11176v1):
    Already uses structured assumptions as lists, proof methods,
    algorithm complexity claims, and richer edge types (improves,
    algorithm_for). This is the nearest concrete model.


B.3. PROPOSED NODE HIERARCHY (the containment tree)
================================================================================

Below, indentation shows containment (parent -> children).
[square brackets] show "zero or more" lists.
{curly braces} show alternatives.

Paper
  paper_id: str
  title: str
  authors: List[str]
  abstract: str
  arxiv_categories: List[str]
  [Section]                                           # optional doc structure
  [MathBlock]                                         # top-level math objects
  [Citation]                                          # bibliography entries

Section
  number: str                  "1", "2.3", "A"
  title: str
  [MathBlock]                  objects in this section
  [Section]                    subsections (recursive)

MathBlock = one of:
  TheoremBlock | DefinitionBlock | ProofBlock | AlgorithmBlock |
  RemarkBlock | ExampleBlock | MiscBlock

  NOTE: Equations are NEVER top-level blocks. They are always children of
  the block they belong to (theorem, proof, definition, etc.). Equations
  that cannot be attributed to a specific block go under a MiscBlock.

TheoremBlock                   # theorem, lemma, proposition, corollary, ...
  id: str
  kind: str                    "theorem" | "lemma" | "proposition" | ...
  label: str | None            LaTeX \label
  number: str | None           "3.1", "A.2" -- visible numbering in paper
  name: str | None             "Brooks' Theorem", "[Hilton-Milner]"
  raw_latex: str
  clean_text: str
  [Assumption]                 structured assumption list
  [Conclusion]                 structured conclusion list
  [EquationBlock]              display equations inside the statement

  PARENT-CHILD RULE: Assumptions, Conclusions, and Equations are all DIRECT
  children of the TheoremBlock (siblings of each other). The logical "IF
  assumptions THEN conclusions" is implicit in the theorem type.

  SIMPLE CASE: All assumptions apply to all conclusions.
    "If G is planar and 3-connected, then G has a Hamiltonian cycle."
    TheoremBlock T1
      +-- Assumption a1: "G is planar"
      +-- Assumption a2: "G is 3-connected"
      +-- Conclusion c1: "G has a Hamiltonian cycle"
    Here c1.requires is absent (= all assumptions apply to all conclusions).

  COMPOUND CASE: Different conclusions need different assumptions.
    "Let M be symmetric. Then eigenvalues are real. Moreover, if M is
     positive-definite, then eigenvalues are positive."
    TheoremBlock T2
      +-- Assumption a1: "M is symmetric"
      +-- Assumption a2: "M is positive-definite"
      +-- Conclusion c1: "eigenvalues are real"         requires: [a1]
      +-- Conclusion c2: "eigenvalues are positive"     requires: [a1, a2]
    Here c1 only needs a1, while c2 needs both a1 and a2.

  DEFAULT: If a Conclusion has no "requires" field, it depends on ALL
  assumptions (the simple case). This is the common case and allows
  graceful degradation: parsers that can't resolve the mapping just
  omit "requires" and it defaults to "all assumptions".

Assumption
  id: str | None               local ID within the theorem ("a1", "a2", ...)
                               OPTIONAL: only needed when conclusions use
                               "requires" to reference specific assumptions.
  text: str                    e.g. "Let M be a positive-definite matrix"
  formal: str | None           e.g. "M in S^n_{++}" (optional semi-formal)

Conclusion
  id: str | None               local ID within the theorem ("c1", "c2", ...)
  text: str                    e.g. "Then v^T M v >= 0 for all v"
  formal: str | None
  requires: List[str] | None   list of Assumption IDs this conclusion depends on.
                               OPTIONAL: absent means "depends on ALL assumptions"
                               (the default for simple theorems).

DefinitionBlock
  id: str
  kind: "definition"
  label, number, name, raw_latex, clean_text   (as above)
  definiendum: str | None      the thing being defined ("Markov chain")
  genus: str | None            what kind of thing it is ("stochastic process")
  differentia: List[str]       distinguishing properties
  notation_introduced: List[str]  new symbols introduced ("X_n", "P(.|.)")
  [Assumption]                 conditions/context for the definition (OPTIONAL)
  [EquationBlock]              display equations giving the formal definition

  PARENT-CHILD RULE: Assumptions and Equations are all DIRECT children of
  the DefinitionBlock (siblings of each other). Assumptions set the context
  ("Let M be a symmetric matrix"), Equations give the defining formula.
  Not every definition has assumptions -- some are unconditional
  (e.g. "A graph is a pair (V, E) where E is a subset of V x V").

ProofBlock
  id: str
  kind: "proof"
  label, raw_latex, clean_text
  proves: str | None           ID of the theorem this proves (T1, L2, ...)
  strategy: str | None         overall proof technique (see TECHNIQUE VOCABULARY)
  [ProofStep]                  ordered sequence of proof steps (OPTIONAL)
                               If parser can't extract steps, this list is
                               absent or empty -- the proof is just raw_latex +
                               clean_text (an opaque text blob).

ProofStep  (Lamport-style)
  index: int                   1-based step number within parent
                               Nested numbering: parent step 2 has sub-steps
                               2.1, 2.2, ... (represented via nesting, not flat)
  text: str                    plain-language description of this step
  technique: str | None        proof technique used IN THIS STEP (see vocabulary)
                               OPTIONAL. A single proof may use different
                               techniques at different steps (e.g. step 2 uses
                               coupling, step 5 uses pigeonhole).
  assume: List[str] | None     local hypotheses introduced by this step
                               (Lamport ASSUME). E.g. ["Suppose gap(M) < c/n"]
                               OPTIONAL: only filled when the step introduces
                               a local assumption (proof by contradiction,
                               case analysis, induction hypothesis, etc.)
  prove: str | None            local goal of this step (Lamport PROVE).
                               What this step establishes. E.g. "tr(A^2) >= n"
                               OPTIONAL: only filled when goal is explicit.
  justification: str | None    what makes this step valid (ref to lemma, etc.)
  [ProofStep]                  sub-steps (Lamport-style RECURSIVE nesting).
                               A step may have sub-steps that break it down
                               further. Depth is unlimited but parsers will
                               typically produce 1-2 levels.
                               OPTIONAL: absent means this step is atomic.
  [CaseBranch]                 case analysis (exclusive with sub-steps).
                               OPTIONAL: only present for case-analysis steps.
  [EquationBlock]              display equations used in this step. OPTIONAL.

  TECHNIQUE VOCABULARY (used for ProofBlock.strategy and ProofStep.technique):

    Classical logic:
      "contradiction"          assume negation, derive contradiction
      "contrapositive"         prove contrapositive instead
      "direct"                 direct chain of implications
      "constructive"           explicitly construct the claimed object
      "counterexample"         construct example showing a claim is false

    Induction family:
      "induction"              mathematical induction (on integers)
      "strong_induction"       assume for all smaller values
      "structural_induction"   induction on structure (trees, formulas, ...)
      "transfinite_induction"  induction over ordinals
      "well_ordering"          appeal to well-ordering principle

    Combinatorial:
      "case_analysis"          split into exhaustive cases
      "pigeonhole"             pigeonhole principle
      "double_counting"        count the same quantity two ways
      "extremal"               consider extremal element (min/max)
      "greedy"                 greedy algorithm argument

    Probabilistic / analytic:
      "probabilistic_method"   show existence via probability > 0
      "coupling"               couple two random processes
      "moment_method"          bound via moments (first, second, etc.)
      "concentration"          use concentration inequalities
      "epsilon_delta"          standard epsilon-delta argument

    Algebraic / structural:
      "diagonalization"        Cantor-style diagonal argument
      "compactness"            use compactness (topological or logical)
      "zorns_lemma"            apply Zorn's lemma
      "spectral"               eigenvalue/eigenvector argument
      "generating_function"    use generating functions

    This list is extensible. Use free-form strings when no standard
    term fits. The parser may use "unknown" when a technique is used
    but cannot be classified.

  SCOPING RULE (Lamport):
    An assumption introduced in step i is in scope for steps i+1, i+2, ...
    within the same parent. Sub-steps inherit all assumptions from ancestors.
    This mirrors variable scoping in programming.

CaseBranch
  condition: str               "Case 1: r = n", "If k is even", ...
  assume: List[str] | None     additional local assumptions for this case
  [ProofStep]                  steps within this case

AlgorithmBlock
  id: str
  kind: "algorithm"
  label, number, name, raw_latex, clean_text
  input_params: List[str]      descriptions of inputs
  output_params: List[str]     descriptions of outputs
  preconditions: List[str]     required conditions
  postconditions: List[str]    guaranteed conditions
  complexity: str | None       "O(n log n)", "polynomial", ...
  [AlgorithmStep]              ordered steps

AlgorithmStep
  index: int
  instruction: str
  [AlgorithmStep]              sub-steps (for loops, conditionals)

EquationBlock
  id: str
  kind: "equation"
  label: str | None
  number: str | None           visible equation number "(3.1)"
  raw_latex: str
  display_type: str            "env" | "inline" | "display_dollar"
  env_name: str                "equation", "align", "gather", ...

RemarkBlock
  id: str
  kind: "remark" | "example" | "note" | "observation" | "exercise"
  label, name, raw_latex, clean_text
  [EquationBlock]

ExampleBlock
  id: str
  kind: "example" | "counterexample"
  label, name, raw_latex, clean_text
  illustrates: str | None      ID of definition/theorem this exemplifies
  [EquationBlock]

MiscBlock
  id: str
  kind: "misc"
  raw_latex: str | None
  clean_text: str | None
  [EquationBlock]              orphan display equations that don't belong to
                               any specific theorem/proof/definition.
                               Every paper has at most one MiscBlock. It is a
                               catch-all container so equations are never
                               top-level nodes.

Citation
  cite_key: str                LaTeX \cite key (e.g. "Brooks", "AK2")
  target_paper_id: str | None  resolved arXiv/DOI if known
  authors: str | None
  title: str | None
  year: str | None


B.4. PROPOSED EDGE TYPES (the semantic graph)
================================================================================

Edges connect any two nodes at any level of the tree. Each edge is typed.

STRUCTURAL EDGES (follow the containment tree -- can be derived from tree):

  contains          Paper/Section/Block -> child node
                    These are implicit from the tree and do NOT need to be
                    stored as explicit edges. The tree IS the structure.

LOGICAL EDGES (mathematical dependencies):

  proves            Proof -> TheoremBlock
                    The proof proves this theorem. (Directed.)

  uses              A -> B
                    A uses/invokes/references B. Most common edge type.
                    Subtypes in metadata:
                      explicit_ref  (from \ref/\eqref)
                      textual_ref   (from "by Theorem 3" in text)
                      keyword       (from shared terminology, low confidence)
                    A proof step uses a lemma. A theorem uses a definition.

  depends_on        A -> B
                    A requires B as a logical prerequisite.
                    Stronger than "uses": B must be established before A.
                    (E.g., Theorem 3 depends_on Lemma 2 -- you need Lemma 2
                     to state or prove Theorem 3.)

  follows_from      A -> B
                    A is a direct consequence of B.
                    (E.g., Corollary 1 follows_from Theorem 1.)

CONCEPTUAL EDGES (mathematical relationships between results):

  These are SEMANTIC CROSS-LINKS stored in the "relations" list. They are
  NOT part of the containment tree. They connect theorems/lemmas to each
  other, potentially across papers.

  DETECTION: These edges are hard to extract automatically. They require
  comparing assumptions and conclusions across theorem pairs. This is a
  DOWNSTREAM PIPELINE task (not the parser's job). The representation
  supports storing them; the pipeline to populate them comes later.

  FORMAL SETUP:
    Model each theorem as  T: A => C  where:
      A = conjunction of assumptions  (A = a1 AND a2 AND ...)
      C = conjunction of conclusions  (C = c1 AND c2 AND ...)

    "A1 implies A2" (written A1 => A2) means A1 is STRONGER (more restrictive).
    If A1 => A2, then anything satisfying A1 also satisfies A2, so A2 is WEAKER.

    "C2 implies C1" (written C2 => C1) means C2 is STRONGER (more informative).

  Given T1: A1 => C1  and  T2: A2 => C2, the relationship between T1 and T2
  depends on how their assumptions and conclusions compare:

  COMPLETE RELATIONSHIP TABLE:
  ============================================================================
  Assumptions         | Conclusions         | Relationship      | Name
  ============================================================================
  A1 <=> A2 (same)    | C2 => C1 (T2 str.) | T2 strengthens T1 | strengthens
  A1 <=> A2 (same)    | C1 => C2 (T1 str.) | T1 strengthens T2 | (inverse)
  A1 <=> A2 (same)    | C1 <=> C2 (same)   | equivalent         | equivalent_to
  A1 <=> A2 (same)    | incomparable        | incomparable concl | (none)
  --------------------+---------------------+-------------------+-------------
  A1 => A2 (T2 wkr.)  | C2 => C1 (T2 str.) | T2 generalizes T1 | generalizes
  A1 => A2 (T2 wkr.)  | C1 <=> C2 (same)   | T2 weakens assm   | weakens_assm
  A1 => A2 (T2 wkr.)  | C1 => C2 (T1 str.) | trade-off          | (none)
  A1 => A2 (T2 wkr.)  | incomparable        | partial            | (none)
  --------------------+---------------------+-------------------+-------------
  A2 => A1 (T1 wkr.)  | C1 => C2 (T1 str.) | T1 generalizes T2 | (inverse)
  A2 => A1 (T1 wkr.)  | C1 <=> C2 (same)   | T1 weakens assm   | (inverse)
  A2 => A1 (T1 wkr.)  | C2 => C1 (T2 str.) | trade-off          | (none)
  A2 => A1 (T1 wkr.)  | incomparable        | partial            | (none)
  --------------------+---------------------+-------------------+-------------
  incomparable        | any                 | incomparable assm  | (none)
  ============================================================================

  KEY:
    "T2 stronger conclusions" means C2 => C1 (C2 logically implies C1)
    "T2 weaker assumptions"   means A1 => A2 (A1 implies A2, so A2 requires less)
    "trade-off" = one side weaker, other side stronger; neither dominates
    "(none)" = no standard edge type; the theorems are incomparable
    "(inverse)" = the same relationship with T1 and T2 swapped

  WHAT TO CHECK (pipeline logic):
    To determine the relationship between T1 and T2, the pipeline must:
    1. Compare assumptions: do A1 => A2? A2 => A1? Both? Neither?
    2. Compare conclusions: do C1 => C2? C2 => C1? Both? Neither?
    3. Look up the table above to determine the edge type.

    In practice, logical implication is hard to check automatically.
    Heuristics include:
    - Subset check: A1's assumption set is a superset of A2's (more conditions)
    - Quantitative: A1 requires "n >= 100", A2 requires "n >= 10" (weaker)
    - Structural: A1 assumes "connected graph", A2 assumes "graph" (weaker)
    - LLM judgment: ask an LLM "does A1 logically imply A2?"

  NAMED EDGE TYPES:

  generalizes       T2 -> T1
                    T2 has WEAKER assumptions AND at least as STRONG conclusions.
                    Formal: (A1 => A2) AND (C2 => C1), with at least one strict.
                    Means: T2 applies in strictly more situations than T1, and
                    gives at least as good a result.
                    Example: Brooks for list coloring generalizes Brooks for
                    ordinary coloring (weaker assumption: lists instead of colors,
                    same conclusion: proper coloring exists).

  special_case_of   T2 -> T1
                    Inverse of generalizes. T2 is a special case of T1.
                    Formal: (A2 => A1) AND (C1 => C2).

  equivalent_to     T1 <-> T2
                    Logically equivalent (symmetric).
                    Formal: (A1 <=> A2) AND (C1 <=> C2).
                    Note: A1/A2 and C1/C2 don't need to be syntactically
                    identical, just logically equivalent.

  strengthens       T2 -> T1
                    SAME assumptions, STRONGER conclusions.
                    Formal: (A1 <=> A2) AND (C2 => C1) AND NOT (C1 => C2).
                    Example: T1 says "gap >= c/n", T2 says "gap >= c*log(n)/n"
                    under the same conditions. T2 strengthens T1.

  weakens_assumptions  T2 -> T1
                    WEAKER assumptions, SAME conclusions.
                    Formal: (A1 => A2) AND NOT (A2 => A1) AND (C1 <=> C2).
                    Example: T1 requires "sub-gaussian entries", T2 only
                    requires "finite 4th moment" but proves the same bound.

  analogous_to      T1 <-> T2
                    Structurally similar but in DIFFERENT DOMAINS. Unlike the
                    other edge types, this one has no clean formal criterion
                    in terms of A => C implication. It is the only "soft"
                    relationship type.

    Requires a "dictionary" mapping concepts in T1's domain to T2's domain,
    such that assumptions map to assumptions and conclusions to conclusions,
    but neither theorem logically implies the other (they live in different
    mathematical universes). Grounded in Gentner's Structure-Mapping Theory
    (1983) and category-theoretic functors (Baez: "every sufficiently precise
    analogy is yearning to become a functor").

    Examples: Konig <-> Dilworth (graphs <-> posets), FTC <-> Stokes
    (intervals <-> manifolds), CLT <-> Martingale CLT (i.i.d. <-> martingale).

    Edge metadata should include: dictionary mapping, confidence, evidence.

    IMPLEMENTATION NOTE: Detecting analogies automatically is significantly
    harder than the other relationship types (which reduce to checking
    logical implication between assumptions/conclusions). Analogy detection
    will be deferred to last in the pipeline.

  CONCRETE EXAMPLE (for the other edge types):
    T1: "If M is Wigner with sub-gaussian entries and n >= n0,
         then gap(M) >= c/n w.h.p."
    T2: "If M is Wigner with finite 4th moment and n >= 1,
         then gap(M) >= c*log(n)/n w.h.p."

    Assumptions: T2 is WEAKER (finite 4th moment < sub-gaussian; n>=1 < n>=n0)
                 So A1 => A2 (anything satisfying T1's assm satisfies T2's)
    Conclusions: T2 is WEAKER (c*log(n)/n < c/n for large n)
                 So C1 => C2 (T1's bound implies T2's)
    Table lookup: A1 => A2 (T2 weaker assm), C1 => C2 (T1 stronger concl)
                 = "trade-off" (no standard edge type)

    T3: "If M is Wigner with finite 4th moment and n >= 1,
         then gap(M) >= c/n w.h.p."
    vs T1: A1 => A3 (T3 weaker assm), C3 => C1 (same concl)
         = T3 weakens_assumptions relative to T1.
    vs T2: A2 <=> A3 (same assm), C3 => C2 (T3 stronger concl)
         = T3 strengthens T2.

DEFINITIONAL EDGES:

  defines           DefinitionBlock -> Concept
                    This definition defines this concept.

  introduces_notation  DefinitionBlock -> symbol/notation string
                    This definition introduces this notation.

ILLUSTRATIVE EDGES:

  illustrates       ExampleBlock -> DefinitionBlock/TheoremBlock
                    This example illustrates that definition or theorem.

  contradicts       ExampleBlock -> statement
                    Counterexample contradicts a (non-theorem) claim.

CROSS-PAPER EDGES:

  cites             Any node -> Citation
                    This node references that external paper.

  extends           TheoremBlock (paper A) -> TheoremBlock (paper B)
                    Theorem in this paper extends a result from another paper.

  refutes           TheoremBlock -> TheoremBlock (another paper)
                    This result disproves or corrects another paper's claim.

  same_as           Node (paper A) <-> Node (paper B)
                    These are the same mathematical object in different papers.


B.5. CONCRETE EXAMPLE: Brooks' Theorem paper (1805.11176v1)
================================================================================

Current flat representation (what the code produces):

  objects: {T1: {kind: "theorem", raw_latex: "...", clean_text: "..."}, ...}
  relations: [{source: "R1", target: "T1", type: "proves"}, ...]

Proposed hierarchical representation:

  Paper: "1805.11176v1"
  |
  +-- Section 1: "Introduction"
  |
  +-- TheoremBlock T1:
  |     kind: "theorem"
  |     name: "Brooks' Theorem"
  |     number: "Theorem 1"
  |     |
  |     +-- Assumption A1: "k >= 3 is a natural number"
  |     +-- Assumption A2: "G is a graph with Delta(G) <= k"
  |     +-- Assumption A3: "G does not contain a clique on k+1 vertices"
  |     +-- Conclusion C1: "G is k-colorable"
  |
  +-- ProofBlock R1:
  |     proves: T1
  |     strategy: "induction"
  |     |
  |     +-- ProofStep 1:
  |     |     text: "Base case: n <= k, holds trivially"
  |     |     prove: "G with n <= k vertices is k-colorable"
  |     |
  |     +-- ProofStep 2:
  |     |     text: "Reduce to k-regular case"
  |     |     assume: ["G has a vertex v with deg(v) < k"]
  |     |     prove: "G is k-colorable"
  |     |     justification: "delete v, apply induction, extend coloring"
  |     |
  |     +-- ProofStep 3:
  |     |     assume: ["G is k-regular", "G has no (k+1)-clique"]
  |     |     prove: "exists non-adjacent neighbors x, y of some vertex v"
  |     |     justification: "neighborhood of v is not a clique"
  |     |
  |     +-- ProofStep 4:
  |     |     text: "Build maximal path P = v1 v2 ... vr starting from v"
  |     |
  |     +-- ProofStep 5:
  |           text: "Case analysis on whether path covers all vertices"
  |           |
  |           +-- CaseBranch "r = n"
  |           |     assume: ["path P covers all n vertices"]
  |           |     +-- ProofStep 5.1:
  |           |     |     text: "Color v1, v3 same color (non-adjacent)"
  |           |     +-- ProofStep 5.2:
  |           |     |     text: "Apply PathColor along path"
  |           |     |     justification: "uses P1 (PathColor procedure)"
  |           |     +-- ProofStep 5.3:
  |           |           prove: "v2 can be colored (two neighbors share a color)"
  |           |
  |           +-- CaseBranch "r < n"
  |                 assume: ["path P has r < n vertices"]
  |                 +-- ProofStep 5.4:
  |                 |     text: "Identify cycle C containing v"
  |                 +-- ProofStep 5.5:
  |                 |     text: "Color G' = G - C by induction hypothesis"
  |                 +-- ProofStep 5.6:
  |                       text: "Color C using PathColor"
  |                       justification: "uses P1"
  |
  +-- AlgorithmBlock A1:
  |     name: "DFS coloring"
  |     complexity: "O(m+n)"
  |     based_on: R1
  |
  +-- DefinitionBlock D2:
  |     definiendum: "constraint graph"
  |     genus: "graph"
  |     |
  |     +-- Assumption: "given a graph G and list assignment L(v)"
  |     +-- EquationBlock: "F = F(L, G)"
  |
  +-- TheoremBlock T4:
        name: "Correspondence-coloring generalization"
        |
        +-- Assumption: "k >= 3"
        +-- Assumption: "Delta(G) <= k, no (k+1)-clique"
        +-- Assumption: "L(v) list assignment with |L(v)| = k"
        +-- Assumption: "F arbitrary constraint graph for (L, G)"
        +-- Conclusion: "there exists a list coloring of G respecting F"

  Semantic edges:
    R1 --proves--> T1
    R1 --uses--> P1                (PathColor procedure)
    A1 --algorithm_for--> T1
    T4 --generalizes--> T1         (stronger: works for list/corresp. coloring)
    T4 --uses--> D2                (uses constraint graph definition)


B.6. REPRESENTATION FORMAT (how to store it)
================================================================================

RECOMMENDED: Property graph stored as nested JSON.

The containment tree is represented by nesting (children inside parents).
Semantic edges are stored in a separate top-level list.
This is the simplest format that captures both structures.

  {
    "schema_version": "v2.0",
    "paper_id": "1805.11176v1",
    "title": "...",
    "authors": [...],
    "abstract": "...",

    "sections": [                           // OPTIONAL structural grouping
      {
        "number": "1",
        "title": "Introduction",
        "blocks": [...]                     // IDs or inline blocks
      }
    ],

    "blocks": [                             // ALL math blocks, in doc order
      {
        "id": "T1",
        "type": "theorem",
        "label": "Theorem Brooks",
        "number": "Theorem 1",
        "name": "Brooks' Theorem",
        "raw_latex": "...",
        "clean_text": "...",
        "assumptions": [
          {"text": "k >= 3 is a natural number"},
          {"text": "G is a graph with Delta(G) <= k"},
          {"text": "G has no (k+1)-clique"}
        ],
        "conclusions": [
          {"text": "G is k-colorable"}
        ],
        "equations": [...]
      },
      {
        "id": "R1",
        "type": "proof",
        "proves": "T1",
        "strategy": "induction",
        "steps": [                              // OPTIONAL: absent = not extracted
          {
            "index": 1,
            "text": "Base case: n <= k holds trivially",
            "prove": "G with n <= k vertices is k-colorable"
                                                // "assume" omitted: no local hyp
          },
          {
            "index": 2,
            "text": "Reduce to k-regular case",
            "assume": ["G has a vertex v with deg(v) < k"],
            "prove": "G is k-colorable",        // Lamport PROVE
            "justification": "delete v, induction, extend coloring"
          },
          {
            "index": 5,
            "text": "Case analysis on r",
            "cases": [                          // case branches with nested steps
              {
                "condition": "r = n",
                "assume": ["path P covers all n vertices"],
                "steps": [
                  {"index": 1, "text": "Color v1, v3 same color"},
                  {"index": 2, "text": "Apply PathColor",
                   "justification": "uses P1"},
                  {"index": 3,
                   "prove": "v2 can be colored (two neighbors share color)"}
                ]
              },
              {
                "condition": "r < n",
                "assume": ["path P has r < n vertices"],
                "steps": [...]
              }
            ]
          }
        ]
      },
      {
        "id": "MISC",                         // catch-all for orphan equations
        "type": "misc",
        "equations": [                        // equations not belonging to any block
          {"id": "E7", "raw_latex": "$$n \\choose k$$", "display_type": "display_dollar"},
          {"id": "E8", "label": "eq:foo", "raw_latex": "...", "display_type": "env"}
        ]
      }
    ],

    "relations": [                          // SEMANTIC EDGES (cross-links)
      {"source": "R1", "target": "T1", "type": "proves"},
      {"source": "R1", "target": "P1", "type": "uses"},
      {"source": "T4", "target": "T1", "type": "generalizes"},
      {"source": "T4", "target": "D2", "type": "uses"}
    ],

    "citations": [                          // BIBLIOGRAPHY
      {"key": "Brooks", "resolved_id": null, "authors": "R.L. Brooks", ...}
    ]
  }

  NOTE ON EQUATIONS: No EquationBlock ever appears directly in the top-level
  "blocks" list. Every equation is nested inside its parent (TheoremBlock,
  ProofBlock, etc.) or inside the MiscBlock. This keeps the top level clean
  and limited to meaningful mathematical structures.


B.7. ALTERNATIVE REPRESENTATIONS CONSIDERED
================================================================================

  Option A: Property Graph (Neo4j-style)
    Every node (including Assumption, ProofStep, etc.) is a first-class
    graph node with properties. Containment is a "CONTAINS" edge type.
    Everything is an edge -- no distinction between tree and graph.

    Pro: Uniform query language (Cypher), good for cross-paper queries.
    Con: Loses the natural nesting. Querying "all assumptions of T1"
         requires graph traversal instead of just reading a list.
         Heavy infrastructure (need a graph database).

    VERDICT: Good for a global multi-paper corpus. Not ideal for
    representing a single paper. Consider this for the cross-paper layer.

  Option B: Relational Database (SQL tables)
    Table per entity type: papers, theorems, assumptions, proof_steps, etc.
    Foreign keys for containment. Junction tables for semantic edges.

    Pro: Mature, scalable, well-understood. Can handle millions of papers.
    Con: Impedance mismatch with tree structure. Lots of JOINs.
         Hard to serialize/inspect a single paper.

    VERDICT: Reasonable for a production backend. Not ideal for the
    conceptual representation or for individual paper JSON files.

  Option C: RDF / OWL Ontology
    Every entity is a URI. Every fact is a subject-predicate-object triple.
    Classes defined in OWL. Reasoning via SPARQL + inference rules.

    Pro: Formal, interoperable, standard. Can link to Wikidata/DBpedia.
    Con: Very verbose. Simple queries become complex SPARQL. RDF stores
         are slower than property graphs for typical math-paper queries.

    VERDICT: Worth considering for interoperability with external ontologies
    (Wikidata, MMLKG). Not the primary representation.

  Option D: Nested JSON (the proposed approach)
    Tree via nesting, graph via a separate relations list.

    Pro: Simple, human-readable, easy to serialize/deserialize, works
         with the existing Python codebase. No database needed.
    Con: Cross-paper queries require loading multiple files.

    VERDICT: Best for single-paper representation and for the Python
    pipeline. Can export to Neo4j/SQL/RDF later if needed.

  Recommendation: Use Option D (nested JSON) as the primary format.
  Consider Option A (Neo4j) as a secondary store for cross-paper queries.


B.8. DECISIONS FOR THE PROJECT OWNER
================================================================================

The following are design choices where multiple valid options exist.
I lay out the options; you decide.

DECISION 1: How deep should proof structure go?

  DECIDED: Lamport-style (deep) with graceful degradation.
  The schema supports full Lamport-style nested steps with ASSUME/PROVE
  per step, justification references, and recursive sub-steps. But the
  parser may produce anything from Level 0 (no steps at all) to Level 3
  (full Lamport). See B.1 principle 5 for the degradation ladder.

DECISION 2: Should sections be first-class nodes?

  (a) YES: Sections are nodes in the tree. Theorems live inside sections.
      Preserves document structure. Useful for "in which section is this?"

  (b) NO: Sections are just metadata tags on blocks. Flat list of blocks.
      Simpler. Most math queries don't care about sections.

  (c) OPTIONAL: Schema supports sections, parser populates when easy.

  MY RECOMMENDATION: (c). Many papers don't have clean section structure.

DECISION 3: How to handle inline definitions?

  Many papers define concepts in running text ("We say that X is Y if...")
  without a \begin{definition} environment. Options:

  (a) Only extract explicit \begin{definition} environments.
      Misses most definitions in many papers.

  (b) Extract inline definitions as DefinitionBlocks with a flag
      "source: inline" and lower confidence.
      Current parser already does this to some extent.

  (c) Treat inline definitions as a separate lighter-weight type
      ("InlineDefinition") that doesn't require full substructure.

  MY RECOMMENDATION: (b). Same type, flagged by source.

DECISION 4: Equation granularity

  DECIDED: All display equations ($$...$$ and \begin{equation} etc.) are
  kept as EquationBlock nodes, but they are NEVER top-level blocks.
  They are always children of the block they belong to. Equations that
  cannot be attributed to a specific parent block go under a single
  MiscBlock catch-all node. Inline math ($...$) stays as text content
  within parent nodes and is not extracted as separate nodes.

DECISION 5: Cross-paper representation

  (a) Each paper is fully independent. Cross-paper links are separate
      files or a separate cross-paper graph layer.

  (b) A single global graph containing all papers, with cross-paper
      edges as first-class relations.

  MY RECOMMENDATION: (a) for storage (one JSON per paper), with a
  separate cross-paper index file or graph database for (b).

DECISION 6: Confidence / provenance on everything?

  Should every field (not just edges) carry a confidence score and a
  source tag (manual, heuristic, LLM, gold)?

  (a) YES: Every field has {"value": ..., "confidence": 0.9, "source": "llm"}.
      Verbose but honest about uncertainty.

  (b) NO: Only edges have confidence. Node fields are best-effort.

  (c) HYBRID: A top-level "provenance" block says which parser/LLM
      produced the extraction, plus per-edge confidence.

  MY RECOMMENDATION: (c).


B.9. CAVEATS AND OPEN QUESTIONS
================================================================================

1. VARIABILITY ACROSS MATHEMATICAL FIELDS

   - Algebra/combinatorics papers: many short theorems with clean
     assume/conclude structure. Proofs often by induction or case analysis.
     The proposed representation fits well.

   - Analysis/PDE papers: theorems have long chains of technical conditions.
     Proofs are "soft" -- long chains of estimates, not discrete steps.
     Proof steps are harder to delineate.

   - Applied math / statistics: may have more algorithms, fewer proofs.
     Might need numerical experiment blocks.

   - Geometry: heavy use of diagrams/figures, which we don't represent.

   The schema should tolerate sparseness -- some block types will be empty
   or absent for certain paper styles.

2. AMBIGUITY IN ASSUMPTION vs. CONCLUSION

   Many theorems don't cleanly separate into "assumptions" and "conclusions."
   For example: "If A and B, then C unless D, in which case E."
   Or: "The following are equivalent: (i) X; (ii) Y; (iii) Z."
   The schema handles (i)/(ii)/(iii) as conclusions, but edge cases exist.

3. PROOF STRUCTURE IS RARELY EXPLICIT

   Very few papers write proofs with explicit numbered steps. Most proofs
   are running paragraphs where the "steps" are implicit. Extracting
   proof steps reliably requires an LLM -- heuristic parsing will get
   poor results for most papers.

4. THE PARSING GAP

   The schema describes what a perfectly annotated paper looks like. The
   automated parser will fill maybe 30-40% of the fields for a typical
   paper. The LLM pipeline might reach 60-70%. Only manual annotation
   approaches 100%. This is by design: the schema is aspirational.

5. THEOREM NUMBERING IS CONTEXT-DEPENDENT

   "Theorem 3.1" in one paper and "Theorem 3.1" in another are unrelated.
   Cross-paper identity requires resolving to canonical mathematical
   concepts (via Wikidata, concept registry, or statement matching).

6. VERSION COMPATIBILITY

   If we change the schema, old JSON files become incompatible. A
   "schema_version" field plus a migration script is essential.


B.10. MIGRATION PATH: current code -> proposed representation
================================================================================

The existing PaperGraph can evolve incrementally:

Phase 1 (small changes to current code):
  - Add assumptions/conclusions as List[Span] to TheoremLike    [DONE]
  - Add ProofStep dataclass and steps: List[ProofStep] to Proof
  - Add strategy field to Proof
  - Populate definiendum/genus on Definition where extractable
  - Add number field to all blocks (parsed from LaTeX counters)

Phase 2 (medium changes):
  - Make children lists explicit (blocks have .children or typed sublists)
  - Add Section nodes with containment
  - Add Citation nodes from \cite{} extraction
  - Serialize as nested JSON instead of flat objects dict

Phase 3 (requires LLM or deep parsing):
  - Extract structured proof steps from running-text proofs
  - Extract structured assumptions as lists (not single blob)
  - Identify proof strategies (induction, contradiction, etc.)
  - Populate cross-paper edges via citation resolution

Phase 4 (corpus-level):
  - Build cross-paper graph layer (Neo4j or separate index)
  - Concept grounding to Wikidata for cross-paper identity
  - Evaluate coverage and accuracy against gold standard


B.11. LAMPORT PROOF STRUCTURE: UNIVERSALITY AND CONVERSION DIFFICULTY
================================================================================

The Lamport-style proof representation (B.3 ProofStep) raises two practical
questions: (a) Can every proof be represented this way? (b) How hard is it
to convert actual LaTeX proofs into this format?

B.11.1. UNIVERSALITY
---------------------

The Lamport format IS universal. Any valid mathematical proof can be decomposed
into a sequence of steps where each step follows from prior steps and known
results. This is a consequence of proof theory: any proof expressible in
natural language corresponds to a derivation in a formal system, which is
inherently sequential and hierarchical. All standard proof techniques map
cleanly:

  Contradiction:  Step 1 ASSUME negation, final step PROVE contradiction.
  Induction:      Step 1 PROVE base case, Step 2 ASSUME P(k) PROVE P(k+1).
  Case analysis:  CaseBranch nodes, each with its own ASSUME + steps.
  Direct:         Sequential steps, each PROVE-ing an intermediate claim.
  Construction:   Steps build the object, final step PROVE-s properties.

However, the quality of decomposition varies by mathematical domain:

  GOOD FIT: Discrete math, combinatorics, algebra -- many short theorems,
  clean logical steps, proofs often by induction or case analysis. The
  Lamport structure adds genuine clarity.

  AWKWARD FIT: Analysis, PDE -- proofs are long chains of estimates
  (A <= B <= C <= ... <= Z) that produce very flat step lists with no
  meaningful nesting. Also "soft" analytic proofs where there are no
  sharp logical breakpoints ("by a standard approximation argument").

  POOR FIT: Diagram-dependent proofs (commutative diagrams, geometric
  constructions) where reasoning is spatial/visual. Also computer-assisted
  proofs (Four Color Theorem) where the "proof" includes a massive
  computation not decomposable into human-readable steps.

None of these are counterexamples to universality -- they can all technically
be Lamport-ized -- but for flat estimate chains the added structure has
little value.

B.11.2. CONVERSION DIFFICULTY (LaTeX -> Lamport)
-------------------------------------------------

Difficulty of converting actual proofs depends on writing style:

  Tier 1 -- EASY (~10-15% of proofs):
    Already structured: numbered steps, "Step 1:", "Claim:", "Case 1:".
    Clear markers: "Assume", "Suppose", "We need to show", "Therefore".
    Heuristic parser with keyword matching gets most of the structure.

  Tier 2 -- MEDIUM (~30-40% of proofs):
    Structured paragraphs with implicit steps. Each paragraph is roughly
    one logical step. Sentence-level splitting at "By/Since/Therefore"
    boundaries works. LLM prompted with "decompose this proof" does well.

  Tier 3 -- HARD (~40-50% of proofs):
    Running prose, no clear step boundaries, multiple logical moves per
    sentence, implicit steps ("clearly", "it is easy to see"). Requires
    understanding mathematical content. LLM can attempt but accuracy drops.

  Tier 4 -- VERY HARD (~5-10% of proofs):
    Proofs relying on diagrams, long computations, domain-specific
    conventions, or multi-page arguments with forward/backward references.
    Requires specialized math-reasoning model or human annotation.

Realistic expectations for our pipeline:
  - Heuristic parser: Level 0-1 for most proofs (raw text + strategy label).
    Occasionally Level 2 for well-structured proofs.
  - LLM pass: Pushes Tier 1-2 proofs to Level 2-3. For Tier 3 proofs,
    produces Level 2 (flat steps without reliable assume/prove).
  - Full Lamport (Level 3): Achievable only for well-structured proofs or
    with LLM assistance + human review.

This is exactly why the graceful degradation design (B.1 principle 5) is
essential: most proofs will NOT reach Level 3 automatically.

B.11.3. RELATED WORK: LLM PROOF DECOMPOSITION (2024-2025 landscape)
---------------------------------------------------------------------

There is NO existing benchmark for "mid-level" Lamport-style proof
extraction. The literature splits into two camps with a gap between them:

  CAMP 1: Full formalization (to Lean/Coq/Isabelle)
    Many benchmarks exist (miniF2F, PutnamBench, ProofNet) that measure
    whether an LLM can produce a machine-checkable proof. These go all
    the way to formal verification -- much deeper than our target.

  CAMP 2: Final-answer evaluation
    Most math reasoning benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH, AIME) evaluate only the
    final numerical answer, ignoring intermediate reasoning quality.

  THE GAP: Semi-structured proof decomposition
    No benchmark specifically measures the ability to convert a natural-
    language proof into structured steps with assume/prove annotations --
    i.e. exactly the mid-level representation we need. The UC Berkeley
    2025 report explicitly identifies this gap: "a significant gap in
    evaluating the generation of novel, natural language mathematical
    proofs... natural language proofs lack the immediate verifiability
    of formal proofs."

The closest related work:

  NaturalProofs (Welleck et al., NeurIPS 2021):
    32k theorem statements and proofs from ProofWiki/Stacks Project.
    Natural language, not Lamport-structured. Benchmarks reference
    retrieval and generation, not step decomposition. Closest existing
    dataset to our needs, but lacks step-level annotation.
    https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.01112

  Chain of States / CoSProver (Wang et al., Dec 2025):
    Proposes extracting a "Chain of States" -- intermediate formal proof
    states aligned with informal reasoning -- as a bridge between natural
    language and Lean4 tactics. The CoS is conceptually similar to our
    Lamport steps (sequence of intermediate goals), but targets formal
    verification rather than semi-structured representation.
    https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.10317

  DeepTheorem (Zhang et al., May 2025):
    121K IMO-level informal theorems/proofs with process evaluation
    metrics (logical validity 40%, completeness 30%, correctness 20%,
    clarity 10%). Uses LLM-as-judge for step quality. Closest to
    evaluating proof step quality in natural language, but evaluates
    LLM-generated proofs, not extraction from existing papers.
    https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.23754

  Hilbert (Apple, Sep 2025):
    Recursive proof decomposition into subgoals: given a theorem, split
    into sub-theorems, prove each, assemble. Achieves 99.2% on miniF2F.
    The recursive decomposition is structurally similar to Lamport
    nesting, but operates in Lean4, not natural language.
    https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.22819

  DRIFT (Zhang et al., Oct 2025):
    Decomposes informal math statements into sub-components for targeted
    retrieval from formal libraries. The decomposition stage is relevant
    (breaking a theorem into atomic concept-focused sub-queries), but
    the goal is autoformalization, not proof structuring.
    https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.10815

OPPORTUNITY: A benchmark for semi-structured proof extraction -- taking
a corpus of LaTeX proofs and measuring how well systems decompose them
into Lamport-style steps (with assume/prove/justification) -- appears to
be a gap in the literature. This could be a contribution of this project
if we build a gold-standard annotated corpus. The NaturalProofs dataset
(32k proofs from ProofWiki) would be a natural starting point for
annotation.
